About

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Political Science at the Maxwell School of Citizenship and Public Affairs, Syracuse University, expecting to defend in 2027. My dissertation, Rewiring the International Monetary System, asks how coalitions form around state-led digital cross-border payment networks, and what those coalitions imply for the structure of monetary power.

The project treats digital currencies not simply as new forms of money but as payment systems — informational infrastructures that generate, route, and govern transaction data. That shift in framing is what connects my work on domestic fintech regulation to my work on international monetary hierarchy: in both cases, the political stakes lie in who occupies the intermediary position and who can see the flows.

My research is supported by an APSA–NSF Doctoral Dissertation Research Improvement Grant and draws on fieldwork with central bankers and payment industry practitioners in Hong Kong and Singapore, with additional fieldwork in the European Union planned for 2027. I am on the academic job market in the 2026–27 cycle.

Research

Peer-Reviewed Publications

Deflected Disruption: Payments and the Taming of Chinese Fintech

With Colin Chia · Forthcoming, New Political Economy

Why and how do state authorities seek to deflect and harness technological disruption? We examine how Chinese authorities handled the rise of platform-based fintech at home and its expansion into Southeast Asia. We argue that intermediation — and the informational power derived from it — explains the trajectory of fintech disruption: authorities accommodated disruption where it served payment-system modernization and growth, but acted strongly to deflect it once it compromised their intermediary positions. In Southeast Asia, intense homegrown competition and defensive regulation similarly contained the disruptive potential of Chinese fintech firms.

Governing Monetary Flows: Informational Constraints and the Politics of Central Bank Digital Currencies Job Market Paper

Under review

Why do states pursue central bank digital currencies? I argue that CBDC adoption responds to informational constraints — limits on a state's ability to govern and benefit from monetary information. Domestically, states seek to recover informational oversight lost to private fintech and cryptocurrency platforms; internationally, they seek to reduce dependence on U.S.-centric financial infrastructure and exposure to financial sanctions. Using a global panel covering 2008–2024 and repeated-event survival analysis, I find that domestic bans on private cryptocurrencies are the strongest predictor of initial CBDC interest, while exposure to U.S. financial sanctions accelerates progression toward implementation.

Book / Dissertation Project

Rewiring the International Monetary System: The Political Economy of Central Bank Digital Currencies

Dissertation and book project

At least sixteen state-led digital cross-border payment networks (DCPNs) are currently under development, with Hong Kong, the European Union, and Singapore emerging as hubs — even as the United States distances itself from the public DCPN agenda in favor of firm-led stablecoins. This project asks how such coalitions emerge and evolve under geopolitical rivalry and technological diffusion. I build an original network dataset of DCPN initiatives, test how far these ties overlap with existing trade, alliance, and regulatory networks, and assess whether emerging digital networks reproduce or diverge from established monetary hierarchies.
